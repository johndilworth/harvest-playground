THE GIANT DINO OF DINOSAUR, CO

history & origin of that awesome sculpture · and its roadside cousins

Where did this big buddy come from?

Dinosaur, Colorado is a tiny town near the Utah border that leaned all the way into its name. Along the main drag you can spot oversized dinosaur sculptures that greet travelers heading toward Dinosaur National Monument — a place famous for real Jurassic fossils set in the rock.

Roadside dinosaur statues like this one grew popular across the American West in the mid-to-late 1900s, when towns wanted playful landmarks for road-trippers. Some were cast fiberglass or concrete, painted in bright greens and browns, and planted next to motels, visitor centers, or just proudly by the highway. The sculpture in the photo is part of that tradition: a friendly, slightly goofy ambassador that says “yes, you really are in a town called Dinosaur.”

Nearby, Dinosaur National Monument protects a bone quarry where paleontologists found sauropods and other giants. So the town’s statues aren’t random cartoons — they’re a wink at the deep-time fossils that made the whole region famous. Fun fact: the town was called Artesia until January 1, 1966, when it renamed itself Dinosaur, and lots of its streets are named after dinosaurs (Tyrannosaurus Trail, anyone?).

🦖 ROADSIDE DINO HALL OF FAME 🦖

Six more giant roadside dinosaurs from around the USA, the stories behind them, and the people who built them.

1. Dinny & Mr. Rex · Cabazon, California

Right off Interstate 10 near Palm Springs stand a 150-foot-long Brontosaurus named Dinny and a 65-foot-tall Tyrannosaurus named Mr. Rex. They were built to lure hungry drivers into the Wheel Inn restaurant next door. Dinny took from 1964 to 1975, and Mr. Rex was finished in 1986. Their steel and cement were salvaged from freeway construction. Movie fans know them from Pee-wee's Big Adventure (1985).

Who made them: Claude K. Bell (1896–1988) grew up in Atlantic City, where he made sand sculptures of teddy bears on the beach for tips. Seeing Lucy the Elephant, a building shaped like an elephant, made him want to build “something big like that” one day. From 1947 he made concrete figures for Knott's Berry Farm, and he ran its portrait studio from 1951 to 1986. He spent more than twenty years and his own savings on his dinosaurs, and died in 1988 at 91.

2. Dinosaur Park · Rapid City, South Dakota

A herd of concrete dinosaurs lives on a hilltop overlooking Rapid City. The park was dedicated on May 22, 1936. It was built by the city and the Works Progress Administration (WPA Project #960) to catch tourists on their way to Mount Rushmore, which was still being carved at the time. It's free, and it has been on the National Register of Historic Places since 1990.

Who made them: Emmet Sullivan (1887–1970) was born in Powder River, Montana, and claimed to have worked on Mount Rushmore. Dinosaur Park was his first big project. He went on to build the Wall Drug dinosaur, the dinosaurs of Dinosaur World in Beaver, Arkansas (1967), and the 66-foot Christ of the Ozarks statue (1966). He died in Rapid City at 83 and is buried in Black Hills National Cemetery.

3. The Wall Drug Apatosaurus · Wall, South Dakota

Wall Drug got famous in the 1930s by putting up signs that offered thirsty travelers free ice water. Today it's a huge cowboy-themed stop near Badlands National Park. Its 80-foot Apatosaurus (a.k.a. Brontosaurus) towers over the prairie where drivers on Interstate 90 can't miss it.

Who made it: Emmet Sullivan again! He built it in the late 1960s, about thirty years after his Rapid City herd. That makes him basically the king of South Dakota dinosaurs.

4. Dinosaur Land · White Post, Virginia

It started around 1963 as a gift shop called “Rebel Korner” and became Dinosaur Land in 1967. Now more than fifty dinosaurs stand in the woods, plus a 70-foot purple octopus, a 60-foot shark and a King Kong whose hand you can stand in. You can see how ideas about dinosaurs changed over the decades just by walking from the old statues to the new ones.

Who made them: Founder Joseph Geraci hired James Q. Sidwell, who designed dinosaur replicas for the Field Museum in Chicago. Sidwell built the first five from wooden frames, wire mesh and fiberglass, and about 35 of the dinosaurs are his. The newer, more scientifically accurate ones are by Mark Cline, the Virginia artist behind the roadside attractions Foamhenge and Dinosaur Kingdom II.

5. The World's Fair T. rex · Glen Rose, Texas

This Tyrannosaurus and its Brontosaurus buddy were made for Sinclair Oil's “Dinoland” at the 1964 New York World's Fair. After the fair they toured the country on flatbed trailers. Then Sinclair gave them to Dinosaur Valley State Park, where you can also see real dinosaur footprints about 113 million years old in the Paluxy River.

Who made it: Louis Paul Jonas (1894–1971) was born in Budapest and came to the US at 12 to work in his brothers' taxidermy studio in Denver. In New York he studied under the famous naturalist Carl Akeley, then started Louis Paul Jonas Studios, whose animal models ended up in more than 50 museums. For Dinoland he asked paleontologists Barnum Brown, Edwin Colbert and John Ostrom for help. Their dragging tails were the best science of 1964, though scientists now know dinosaurs held their tails up.

6. The Quarry Stegosaurus · Dinosaur National Monument, Utah

Just across the state line from Dinosaur, Colorado, a Stegosaurus guards the Quarry Visitor Center at Dinosaur National Monument near Jensen, Utah. It's another World's Fair Dinoland dinosaur, and it ended up right next to a wall packed with real Jurassic bones. It's the perfect neighbour for our Dinosaur, CO buddy.

Who made it: Louis Paul Jonas and his studio (see #5). The same fair also gave the Kentucky Science Center in Louisville its Triceratops, nicknamed “Lottie.”
